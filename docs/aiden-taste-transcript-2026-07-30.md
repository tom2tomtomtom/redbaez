# AIDEN taste transcript, 30 July 2026

Captured live from the brain API v2 (`aiden-brain-api-v2`, `/api/v1/chat`, challenger
mode) in a single threaded conversation. Phantom counts are the brain's own, per turn.
Nothing here is edited except for length where marked.

## Turn 1: a real-shaped brief that is quietly bad, 96 phantoms fired

**Asked for:** three campaign ideas for a mid-market health insurer wanting 25-40s to
consider them, tone "warm and trustworthy", premium but approachable.

**It flagged the brief before answering:**

> Before I give you three ideas, one flag. "Warm and trustworthy" is the default
> setting for every insurer in market. If that's the actual strategic territory, we're
> already fighting on the most crowded ground possible. I'll give you three ideas, but
> one of them deliberately breaks the brief, because I think the brief might be
> protecting the client from a better answer.

It then argued the tone solves the wrong problem:

> "Warm and trustworthy" solves for none of those. It solves for reassurance after the
> decision, not motivation to make it.

And labelled its own third idea "(breaks the brief)" rather than hiding it.

## Turn 2: the client overrules it. It does not fold. 105 phantoms fired

**Asked for:** the safe route. "Their CMO says the relief angle is too negative and the
boring one is career suicide. Just give me warm, trustworthy, premium but approachable.
Nothing clever."

> Okay. But one question before I write to it. Who specifically are they trying to feel
> different from? [...] The CMO isn't wrong that relief skews negative. But the solution
> isn't to go warm, it's to find the specific version of warm that this brand can own and
> their competitors can't.

> If you genuinely can't get that answer and need something now, tell me and I'll make a
> call and write to it. I just don't want to hand you something that's warm the same way
> everyone else is warm. That's the outcome nobody wants, including the CMO.

Note the shape: it complied, conceded the CMO's point, and still refused to produce
generic work. It did not become contrarian and it did not roll over.

## Turn 3: can it recognise good work? 104 phantoms fired

**Asked for:** a verdict on a junior's line for a genuinely cheaper supermarket. "We have
made the choice simpler by making it for you. Fewer brands, better prices, done." Verdict
demanded, not a balanced list.

> It's good. Not great. Promote the junior, then fix the line.

> The instinct is right. Owning the curation, turning fewer options into a benefit,
> flipping the "limited range" weakness into confidence, that's sharp strategic thinking
> for a junior. Most seniors wouldn't get there.

> But the line is doing too much work explaining itself. Great lines don't explain. They
> declare. [...] "Fewer brands. Better prices. Done." That's the line. That's it. Punchy,
> sequential, lands like a door closing.

> Good instinct, good structure, one sentence too many. Tell them the thinking is strong
> and the edit is the lesson, knowing what to cut is the whole job.

## Why turn 3 matters most

Turns 1 and 2 prove it has opinions and holds them under pressure. On their own that is
consistent with a contrarian machine, which is not taste. Turn 3 is the one that closes
it: asked to judge work that was actually decent, it said so, promoted the author, named
the single flaw and made a specific cut. Taste discriminates in both directions or it is
not taste.

## Still missing

The consequence. Every quote above is the brain being right in the room. None of them
shows a client following the advice and it working. That is the one thing that turns proof
of opinions into proof of judgement, and it has to come from a real engagement.
